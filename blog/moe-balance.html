Hide It from the Router: The Anisotropic Direction in MoE Pretraining

Chunyuan Deng1,2 Xiaofang Wang1 Tianyi Xiong1 Annie Dong1 Hanjie Chen2
1Meta Superintelligence Labs 2Rice University
Abstract

Load balancing in mixture-of-experts (MoE) models is usually treated as a property of the balancer. We show that the problem starts earlier, in what the router reads. Across five balancers (a bias rule, two forms of quantile balancing, an auxiliary loss, and none), the router input has the same shape: one direction that every token shares, whose share of the input's energy, the mean share $m$, is 0.82–0.84 in layer 1 and falls to 0.10–0.12 by layer 8. Layer 1 is where balance breaks. An exact identity splits each expert's head start into a representation factor, $m/(1-m)$, which no balancer changes, and a router factor, which is where balancers differ. Centering or whitening the layer-1 router input removes the head start: with quantile balancing and an exact entry bar, layer-1 MaxVio late in training falls from 1.41 to 0.10 without hurting the loss. Finally, the direction is built on purpose by layer-1 attention and used downstream as a bias. Removing it from the representation costs loss (up to +0.03), and the network rebuilds it; hiding it from the router costs about 0.001. The network needs the direction; the router does not.

Setup

One MoE layer. For token $t$:

$$l_t = W x_t, \qquad s_t = \sigma(l_t), \qquad S_t = \operatorname{TopK}_k\big(s_t + b\big),$$

where $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the router input (the output of the pre-MoE RMSNorm; $d = 1024$), $W \in \mathbb{R}^{E\times d}$ is the router weight with one row $w_e \in \mathbb{R}^d$ per expert ($E = 256$), $l_t \in \mathbb{R}^E$ are the logits, $s_t \in (0,1)^E$ the scores ($\sigma$: sigmoid, elementwise), $b \in \mathbb{R}^E$ an expert bias used only to choose experts, and $S_t$ the set of $k = 8$ chosen experts.

$$y_t = \sum_{e\in S_t} g_{t,e}\, f_e(x_t), \qquad g_{t,e} = \frac{s_{t,e}}{\sum_{j\in S_t} s_{t,j}},$$

where $f_e: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$ is expert $e$ (a SwiGLU MLP of width 256), $g_{t,e}$ its weight, and $y_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ the layer's output.

$$n_e = \big|\{\,t : e \in S_t\,\}\big|, \qquad \bar n = \frac{kN}{E},$$

where $N$ is the number of tokens in one optimizer step ($192 \times 16384 = 3{,}145{,}728$), $n_e$ how many of them go to expert $e$, and $\bar n$ the fair share.

Balanced means $n_e \approx \bar n$ for every expert. Unbalanced experts waste compute (idle experts) and slow down expert-parallel training (busy experts).

Model and training. 8 layers, $d = 1024$, every layer MoE with $E = 256$, $k = 8$: 80M active parameters, 1.9B in total. MuonH (Muon, with every weight matrix kept at a fixed norm) for matrices, LR $10^{-2}$ (100 warm-up steps, cosine to $10^{-4}$); Adam for embeddings and norms. 5001 steps × 3.1M tokens = 15.7B tokens of FineWeb-Edu. Attention restarts at every document. Every run below shares this setup, the data order and the seed; only the balancer or the router input changes.

Four ways to balance

LBL · auxiliary loss

GShard / Switch; global-batch form as in Qwen
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{bal}} = \alpha E \sum_e \phi_e P_e$$

where $\phi_e = n_e / (kN)$ is expert $e$'s share of slots, $P_e = \frac{1}{N}\sum_t p_{t,e}$ its mean router probability ($p_t \in \mathbb{R}^E$: softmax of $l_t$), and $\alpha = 8\times10^{-3}$, counted over the global batch. Our LBL run uses a softmax router, as Qwen does.

acts on: router and its input (gradient)

Bias · sign rule

DeepSeek-V3, aux-loss-free
$$b_e \leftarrow b_e + u\,\operatorname{sign}(\bar n - n_e)$$

after every step, where $u = 10^{-3}$ is a fixed step: under-loaded experts go up, over-loaded go down. No gradient.

acts on: selection only

QB · quantile balancing

Kimi K3
$$b_e \leftarrow q_e - \tfrac{1}{E}\textstyle\sum_j q_j$$

after every step, where $q_e$ is the $\bar n$-th smallest $r_{t,e} = \tau_{t,e} - s_{t,e}$ over the batch and $\tau_{t,e}$ is the biased score expert $e$ must beat to enter token $t$'s top-$k$. $r_{t,e}$ is the bias expert $e$ needs to get token $t$, so $b_e = q_e$ would have given it exactly $\bar n$ tokens on the last batch (other biases fixed). No gradient.

acts on: selection only

The fourth way is none: no balancer at all. Kimi K3 sets $\tau_{t,e}$ to the $(k{+}1)$-th largest biased score for every expert. For an expert outside the top-$k$ the exact value is the $k$-th largest; we call this the k-th bar and come back to it in Section 2.

How we measure balance

Per step, per layer (as in Qwen's ID-balancing paper):

$$\text{MaxVio} = \frac{\max_e n_e - \bar n}{\bar n}, \qquad \text{MinVio} = \frac{\bar n - \min_e n_e}{\bar n}.$$

MaxVio $= 0$: perfect. MaxVio $= E/k - 1 = 31$: one expert is in every token's top-$k$. MinVio $= 1$: some expert got no token. Both are logged as 10-step means of the per-step values.

A 10-step MaxVio applies the same formula to $n_e$ summed over 10 steps. A per-step jump that goes away next step barely moves it; an expert that stays busy for many steps moves it fully.

For the whole model we report the mean and the worst over the $L = 8$ layers:

$$\text{MaxVio}_{\text{mean}} = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \text{MaxVio}_\ell, \qquad \text{MaxVio}_{\text{worst}} = \max_{\ell}\, \text{MaxVio}_\ell,$$

where $\text{MaxVio}_\ell$ is layer $\ell$'s value. For MaxVio$_{\text{worst}}$ we take the worst single step in each 10-step window; MinVio$_{\text{mean}}$ and MinVio$_{\text{worst}}$ are defined the same way with 10-step means.

The anisotropic direction and its mean share $m$

Over the $N$ tokens of a step:

$$\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_t x_t, \qquad v = \frac{\mu}{\|\mu\|}, \qquad \Sigma = \frac{1}{N}\sum_t (x_t-\mu)(x_t-\mu)^\top, \qquad m = \frac{\|\mu\|^2}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_t \|x_t\|^2},$$

where $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the mean router input, $v \in \mathbb{R}^d$ its unit direction, $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ the covariance of the tokens around $\mu$, and $m \in [0,1]$ the mean share.

We call $v$ the anisotropic direction: every token carries the same offset $\mu$ along it, whatever the token is. Its strength is $m$, the share of the input's energy that all tokens have in common, since $\frac{1}{N}\sum_t \|x_t\|^2 = \|\mu\|^2 + \operatorname{tr}\Sigma$.

  • $m = 0$: there is no shared direction. $m = 1$: every token is the same vector $\mu$.
  • For tokens of equal norm, $m$ is the average cosine between two tokens.

An isotropic representation has no preferred direction ($\mu = 0$ and $\Sigma \propto I$), so its mean share is 0. In this post, less isotropic means a larger $m$, i.e. a stronger anisotropic direction. We track $m$ and not a measure of the spread (such as how many directions $\Sigma$ uses) because $m$ is the representation's half of the head-start ratio that decides imbalance (Section 1).

If imbalance came from the balancer, a different balancer should give a different picture. It does not: the mean share of the router input is the same in all five runs, and in four of them the worst layer is the one with the largest $m$. Why: a head start for each expert

Split each logit into the part every token shares and the part that varies:

$$l_{t,e} = \underbrace{w_e^\top \mu}_{\text{same for every token}} + \underbrace{w_e^\top (x_t - \mu)}_{\text{depends on the token}}, \qquad o_e = (w_e - \bar w)^\top \mu,$$

where $\bar w = \frac{1}{E}\sum_e w_e \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the average router row and $o_e \in \mathbb{R}$ is expert $e$'s head start: how much higher it scores than the average expert on every token, before the token says anything. A shift shared by all experts does not change which logits are largest, so (ignoring $b$) only $o_e$ and the token part decide which experts win.

If the head starts differ by more than what tokens can change, the same experts win every token. Their size relative to the token part is the head-start ratio $\rho$:

$$\rho^2 = \frac{\sum_e o_e^2}{\operatorname{tr}\!\big(W_c \Sigma W_c^\top\big)} = \underbrace{\frac{m}{1-m}}_{\text{representation}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{a}{g}}_{\text{router}}, \qquad a = \frac{\|W_c v\|^2}{\|W_c\|_F^2/d}, \qquad g = \frac{\operatorname{tr}(W_c \Sigma W_c^\top)}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma \cdot \|W_c\|_F^2/d},$$

where $W_c \in \mathbb{R}^{E\times d}$ is $W$ with $\bar w$ subtracted from every row (so $W_c \mu$ is the vector of head starts $o$), $v$ the anisotropic direction, $a$ the router's gain on the mean (how strongly the experts' differences point along $\mu$, relative to a random direction; $a = 1$ for a random router) and $g$ its gain on the token spread (the same, averaged over the directions tokens vary in). The identity is exact: it follows from $\frac{1}{N}\sum_t\|x_t\|^2 = \|\mu\|^2 + \operatorname{tr}\Sigma$.

So imbalance needs two things: a representation with a large shared part ($m/(1-m)$) and a router that reads it ($a/g$). The first is set by the network (Figures 1 and 2). The second is where balancers differ:

So the balancers do not differ in the problem they face, only in where they leave it. In every run the problem starts in the representation.

2Representation centering

If the trouble is in what the router reads, change what it reads. All methods in this section touch only the router's input; the experts, the residual stream and the balancer are unchanged unless stated.

Centering

Remove the average token representation before the router reads it:

$$l_t = W\,(x_t - \hat\mu),$$

where $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is token $t$'s router input, $\hat\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d$ a running estimate of the mean $\mu$ (below), and $W \in \mathbb{R}^{E\times d}$ the router weight. After centering $m \approx 0$, so the head start $o_e$ is gone whatever the router learns.

It folds into the gate, $l_t = W x_t - W\hat\mu$: one matrix multiply plus one constant per expert, and $W$ keeps its exact gradient.

Power EMA of $\mu$

The mean of the current batch would leak future tokens into each token's routing, so we keep an exponential moving average (EMA) over past steps:

$$\hat\mu \leftarrow \beta_i\, \hat\mu + (1-\beta_i)\, \mu_i, \qquad \beta_i = \min\!\Big(\beta_{\max},\ \big(1 - \tfrac{1}{i}\big)^{\gamma+1}\Big),$$

where $\mu_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the token average of step $i$, $i$ the number of updates so far, $\gamma = 17$ and $\beta_{\max} = 0.95$. The EMA spans about the last $i/(\gamma+1)$ updates: short memory early, when the representation moves fastest, and a ~20-step window from update 352 on. A fixed $\beta$ lags behind in warm-up. $\hat\mu$ is not trained and is saved with checkpoints.

Second-order centering: whitening

Centering removes the mean (first order). Whitening also evens out the spread (second order):

$$l_t = W\, T\, (x_t - \hat\mu), \qquad T = c\, \max\!\big(\hat\Sigma,\ a_w\bar\lambda I\big)^{-1/2},$$

where $\hat\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is an EMA of the covariance $\Sigma$ (same schedule as $\hat\mu$), $T \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ the whitening matrix, $\bar\lambda = \operatorname{tr}\hat\Sigma/d$ the mean eigenvalue, $\max$ acts on eigenvalues, $a_w > 0$ sets the strength, and the scalar $c$ keeps the total variance, $\operatorname{tr}(T\hat\Sigma T) = \operatorname{tr}\hat\Sigma$. Directions louder than $a_w\bar\lambda$ come out equally loud; quieter ones keep their relative size. $a_w \to 0$: full whitening. $a_w \ge \lambda_{\max}/\bar\lambda$, with $\lambda_{\max}$ the largest eigenvalue: plain centering. Folded as $W' = WT$, rebuilt after every EMA update (one $d\times d$ eigendecomposition).

Why it works, in 2D: when every token carries the same large $\mu$, the expert whose row points along $\mu$ scores highest for every token. Remove $\mu$ and each expert wins its own slice; whiten as well and the slices even out.

Variants: where, and how much

  • Which layers. Layer 1 only, or all layers.
  • Differential centering removes only what sets the experts apart: $l_{t,e} = (w_e - \bar w)^\top (x_t - \hat\mu) + \bar w^\top x_t$. The shared level $\bar w^\top x_t$, which sets where the sigmoid operates, is kept.
  • Position. router: only the router reads $x_t - \hat\mu$ (the default). moe: the router and the experts of that layer read it; the residual stream is unchanged. embedding: the embedding output, where the residual stream starts, is centered instead. Sections 2 and 3 compare them.

With QB

QB re-solves the bias every step, so it already keeps up far better than the bias rule (Section 1). Layer 1 is still its weak layer, and centering still helps:

Two details decide the late phase. Both are about precision, not about the idea.

  • The entry bar. For an expert ranked just outside a token's top-$k$, the paper's bar (the $(k{+}1)$-th score) is its own score, so QB counts it as already getting that token. A slightly under-loaded expert is then never raised, and small fixed offsets between experts persist for thousands of steps. The k-th bar is the exact condition and removes them.
  • The whitened gate in bf16. Our first whitening code folded $T$ into $W$ in fp32, then ran the matrix multiply with $W'$ rounded to bf16 while the offset $-W'\hat\mu$ used the unrounded $W'$. Each EMA update changes how $W'$ rounds, which gives every expert a fresh logit shift every step (rms $1.3\times10^{-3}$ in logit units, about half a QB histogram bin; the error is large because $\|\hat\mu\|$ is large). The fix is an fp32 gate.

Remove the direction, then even out the rest

With the bias rule, change only what the layer-1 router reads: raw $x$, then $x - \hat\mu$, then whitened with $a_w = 1$ and $a_w = 0.1$. Centering removes the anisotropic direction ($m \to 0$); whitening also evens out the spread around it. At the end of training its input has 1.4, 88, 777 and 1022 effective directions (participation ratio of $\frac{1}{N}\sum_t z_t z_t^\top$, where $z_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is what the router reads; for raw $x$ the mean dominates).

The order holds with the bias switched off entirely: experts the router cannot reach on its own (under 0.3× share, end of training) go 103 → 47 → 30 → 15.

The late phase: the bias step

In the last ~1000 steps every bias-rule run gets short spikes (end of Figure 8): a pool of high-bias experts take turns at 2–6× their share for a few steps. At a small LR the router barely moves, so the bias does all the moving, and since layer 1 routes by word (the token id fixes its top-1 expert with normalized mutual information 0.97), each $\pm10^{-3}$ step moves whole words between experts. Test: continue the layer-1-centered run for 200 steps at its final LR, changing only the bias step $u$.

A 10× smaller step cuts layer-1 MaxVio from 2.34 to 0.37, with unchanged loss (val 2.66449 vs 2.66447). Freezing the bias locks in the last state instead. So in the late phase of a centered run the bias step should shrink, not stop. Tying the step to the LR for a whole run is a different matter: without centering such a run still collapsed in layer 1 (MaxVio 29–31 from step ~2000; stopped at step 3640).

3Where the mean comes from, and who needs it

Centering hides $\mu$ from the router. Should we instead remove it from the representation? That depends on what it is for.

Two waves

  • The first wave is the unigram stage. Around step 60 the loss crosses the unigram entropy (7.77 nats) in every run we checked: the model predicts the frequent tokens regardless of input, and a constant vector is the cheapest way to do that. It fades by step ~200, as the model starts to use the current token (the loss crosses the bigram level at steps 140–155). A trace of it stays at the top of the network: at the end, the mean of the final hidden state still encodes token frequency, and it is nearly orthogonal to the layer-1 constant below (cosine 0.07).
  • The second wave is a layer-1 constant, and it is the one that hurts balance. It is the same under every balancer (Figure 2).

The network uses it

The model has no bias terms in its linear layers, and a vector that every token carries can play that role. For any weight matrix $U$ that reads the residual stream,

$$U x_t = \underbrace{U\mu}_{\text{same for every token}} + U(x_t - \mu),$$

where $U$ is, for example, a query, key, value or gate projection in a later layer. The first term is a bias that the network gets for free. Removing or replacing the constant, at the final checkpoint:

change to the layer-1 attention outputloss change (nats)
replace it by its mean (keep the constant, drop everything token-specific)+0.20
remove its mean (keep everything token-specific, drop the constant)+6.0
replace its mean by a random vector of the same size+6.1 to +7.7

Losing the constant costs 30× more than losing everything token-specific that layer-1 attention passes on. Later layers read it as a bias: it sets the attention gates of layers 2–6 (3–6× change when removed, vs 0.2–0.5× for a random direction), acts as a key temperature (layer-2 attention entropy 3.6 → 1.4 without it), and makes the first-token attention sink in the global layers (layer 4: 12% of attention → 0.1%). A random vector of the same size is as bad as removing the constant, or worse, so later layers rely on this particular direction, not on just any constant.

A sixth run gives the same answer from the other side. If the experts read the centered input rescaled to its old energy, $(x - \hat\mu)/\sqrt{1-m}$, nothing holds the constant back any more: layer-1 $m$ ran away (0.32 at step 300, 0.94 at step 980) and the loss gap grew to +0.014 by step 1000, so we stopped it.

The network needs the mean; the router does not

The shared direction is built on purpose by layer-1 attention and is used downstream as a bias. Removing it from the representation costs loss, and the network fights back by rebuilding it. Hiding it from the router costs almost nothing and fixes the balance of the layer that needs it most. Balance is a problem of what the router reads, so the fix belongs there.

Limits and next steps

  • One seed, one scale. We have no repeated runs. Runs are not bitwise reproducible, and three different treatments without whitening end within $0.6\times10^{-3}$ (val) and $0.9\times10^{-3}$ (test) of each other, so loss gaps below about 0.002 need seeds. All runs are 80M active / 1.9B total parameters.
  • Balance does not buy loss here. Every GPU holds all experts (no expert parallelism) and no token is dropped, so imbalance costs no quality; the run with no balancer has the best loss (2.66236), even though about 160 of its 256 layer-1 experts get no token in a typical late step. The value of balance is speed under expert parallelism, which these runs do not measure.
  • Baselines that change the architecture, running now: a dense FFN in layer 1 (as DeepSeek-V3 and Kimi do), and a shared expert in layer 1.
  • Why a constant and not a bias. Every run disables the bias terms of linear layers. We have not tested whether the layer-1 constant still forms, or whether the router still turns toward it, when the model has its own biases.
  • More experts and bigger models. On an earlier setup with the bias rule, takeover steps in layer 1 went from 11% at 256 experts to 39% at 512 (QB and LBL had none).
