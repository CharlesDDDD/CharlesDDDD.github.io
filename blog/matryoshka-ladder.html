Grow, Don't Restart: A Matryoshka Scaling Ladder and Its Scaling Law

Abstract

A scaling ladder trains a series of models of growing size and fits a law to their losses. We build the ladder differently. Each rung is grown from the trained rung below by an exact expansion that leaves the model's output unchanged, and is then trained on its own token budget. A ladder trains every rung anyway, so this Matryoshka ladder uses the same compute as a ladder trained from scratch. On mixture-of-experts models with 79M to 414M active parameters (8 to 14 layers), with both ladders tuned the same way, grown rungs end with lower test loss than scratch rungs of the same size, by 0.023 to 0.032. Read through the scratch scaling law, a grown rung is worth 1.24–1.28× its compute: about 20% of the compute is saved at every rung. The grown ladder follows its own power law, nearly parallel to the scratch law. Predictions committed before the 14-layer runs started hit both ladders' 14-layer losses within 0.05%, 2.5× beyond the fitted compute. The saving tracks the compute of the rung below: a grown rung is worth its own compute plus 0.6–1.0 times the compute of its donor, and nothing more from earlier rungs.

The idea

To predict how a recipe behaves at large scale, one trains a ladder: the same recipe at a few growing sizes (the rungs), each on a token budget that grows with its size. One fits a power law to the final losses and reads off larger sizes. Every rung starts from random weights, so each rung relearns what the rung below already knows.

A Matryoshka ladder reuses that work. Rung $k{+}1$ starts as the trained rung $k$, expanded to the larger shape so that it computes exactly the same function (its loss right after growth equals the smaller model's), and is then trained on rung $k{+}1$'s full token budget. Each rung contains the one below, like nested dolls. (The name is unrelated to Matryoshka representation learning or MatFormer.)

Both ladders train every rung, on the same tokens per parameter, so they cost the same. If grown rungs end lower, the gain is free. The question this post is about: does the grown ladder still follow a scaling law that predicts its larger rungs? It does not have to follow the scratch law; it needs a law of its own.

We measure the gain at a rung with two numbers:

$$G_k = L^{\text{s}}_k - L^{\text{g}}_k, \qquad R_k = \frac{G_k}{L^{\text{s}}_{k-1} - L^{\text{s}}_k},$$

where $L^{\text{s}}_k$ and $L^{\text{g}}_k$ are the final test losses (nats per token) of the scratch and grown runs at rung $k$, $G_k$ is the gain, and $R_k$ the transfer rate: the gain as a share of what one scratch rung buys. $R = 0$: growth helps nothing. $R = 1$: growth is worth a whole rung.

A third number states the gain as compute. With the scratch scaling law $L^{\text{s}}(C)$ (Section 3), the compute multiplier $M_k$ solves $L^{\text{s}}(M_k C_k) = L^{\text{g}}_k$: the scratch ladder would need $M_k$ times rung $k$'s compute $C_k$ to reach the grown loss, so growth saves a share $1 - 1/M_k$ of the compute.

Setup

Model. A mixture-of-experts (MoE) decoder. Every layer has attention and an MoE block with 128 experts, 8 chosen per token, each expert a quarter of the hidden size wide. Hidden size is $128 \times$ layers; attention heads are 64 wide with 2 key/value heads at every size. Attention uses a sliding window of 512 tokens, except one global layer in every four; output logits are soft-capped at 20. Data: FineWeb-Edu, Llama-3 tokenizer, sequences of 16,384 tokens, attention restarted at every document. Experts are balanced with Quantile Balancing (arXiv 2607.24653): each expert's routing bias is set from quantiles of the router scores so that every expert gets its share of tokens, with no auxiliary loss. (Our first ladder used the common sign-rule bias update; its scratch runs collapsed, Section 8.)

Optimizer. MuonH for weight matrices and AdamH for the output head: Muon and Adam steps with every matrix kept at a fixed norm (on a hypersphere), so no weight decay. Learning rates transfer across depth and token count with HyperP (arXiv 2603.28743): the peak hidden-layer LR is

$$\eta = \eta_{\text{ref}} \sqrt{\frac{8}{L}} \left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{0.32},$$

where $L$ is the number of layers, $T$ the run's training tokens, $T_0 = 15.73$B tokens the reference, and $\eta_{\text{ref}}$ the reference LR, set per ladder in Section 1. 100 warm-up steps, then cosine decay to 1% of the peak.

Budget. Every run trains on 100 tokens per active non-embedding parameter (TPP 100). Every run is scored on the same fixed test set: 3,840 sequences of 16,384 tokens (63M tokens).

Growing a rung exactly

One hop adds layers (8 → 10), hidden width (1024 → 1280), query heads and expert width, while the 128 experts and the 2 key/value heads stay. The grown model computes the same function as the trained small one:

  • New hidden dimensions start at zero. The embedding and every residual write leave them at zero, and the RMSNorm gains are scaled by $\sqrt{h/h'}$ ($h, h'$: old and new width), so the old dimensions are normalized exactly as before.
  • New query heads and new expert neurons read but do not write: their output weights are zero.
  • Old residual branches are scaled by $\sqrt{L'/L}$ ($L, L'$: old and new depth). This undoes the smaller branch multiplier $1/\sqrt{2L'}$ that the recipe gives deeper models.
  • New layers output zero. They are added after the old ones. Their query heads and expert neurons come in pairs with the same input weights and opposite output weights, so each pair cancels. (Plain zero weights do not work: MuonH keeps every matrix at its initial norm, which must not be zero.)

All other new weights (what new parts read, the new layers' inputs) keep the fresh initialization a scratch run would have. Check, growing a trained 10-layer model to 12 layers: on 384 test sequences the loss is 2.55759 before and 2.55761 after growth (difference $2\times10^{-5} \pm 2\times10^{-5}$), the KL divergence from the small model is 0.001, and the top-1 prediction agrees on 98.4% of tokens: the same function up to bf16 rounding.

The grown model then trains like any run: new optimizer state, its own warm-up and cosine schedule, its own batch and LR rule, and the data stream from the start. Ladder data is nested, so the larger rung's tokens include all of the smaller rung's.

1Tuning both ladders the same way

A grown model starts partly trained, so it may want a different LR and batch than a scratch model. Comparing both at one setting would favour whichever ladder it suits. We compare best to best: each ladder gets the same tuning effort, at a small rung, and the rules found there are applied unchanged to the larger rungs.

Grid. The tuning was done with the first ladder's sign-rule balancing; we kept the rules unchanged when we switched to Quantile Balancing, since the balancing method does not move the best LR. At 10 layers, TPP 100, we trained each ladder at batch 36, 72 and 144 and 2–4 learning rates per batch (7 scratch runs, 8 grown runs; every grown run starts from the same trained 8-layer model). We fit a quadratic surface to the final test loss,

$$\ell(x, y) = c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 y + c_3 x^2 + c_4 xy + c_5 y^2,$$

where $x = \ln(\eta_{\text{ref}}/0.012)$ is the log LR multiplier, $y = \ln B$ the log batch ($B$ in sequences), and $c_0, \ldots, c_5$ are least-squares coefficients. The best LR at batch $B$ solves $\partial\ell/\partial x = 0$, which gives a power law in $B$; the best batch is the lowest point along that line.

Do the rules transfer? Two checks at other depths:

  • 8 layers, scratch. The rule predicts the best LR at batch 48 to be ×0.90. An LR scan at batch 48 puts it at ×0.83 (quadratic through 4 LRs).
  • 12 layers, both ladders. Both 0.71× and 1.41× the rule's LR are worse than the rule itself: scratch 2.4248 and 2.4270 vs 2.4191, grown 2.3947 and 2.3956 vs 2.3930. The rule sits at the optimum to within these steps in both ladders.

3Scaling laws and pre-registered predictions

Each ladder gets its own law:

$$L(C) = E + A\, C^{-\alpha}, \qquad C = 6ND,$$

where $L$ is the final test loss, $C$ the training compute in FLOPs, $N$ the active non-embedding parameters, $D$ the training tokens, $E$ an irreducible floor, and $A$, $\alpha$ the scale and slope. The scratch law is fitted to the scratch rungs; the grown law to the grown rungs only (the 8L root is a scratch run).

We wrote down the fitting protocol before the runs it scores, and commit every prediction to git before the rung it predicts starts. The protocol fixes which runs enter (seed replicates are averaged), the law, how $E$ is estimated, the intervals and a pass rule. With fewer than 5 rungs in a ladder the main fit is a pure power law ($E = 0$), as in HyperP; with a free floor both ladders put the best $E$ at its lower bound 0 anyway.

First test: 14 layers. On 18 November, with 8–12L finished, we fitted both ladders (the grown one, with only two rungs, jointly with the scratch one) and committed predictions for 14, 16 and 18 layers. Then the 14L runs started.

4Where the saving comes from

A grown rung starts from the rung below, so a natural guess is that the saving is the compute it reuses. We write the compute a grown rung is worth as its own compute plus a fraction $\kappa$ of its donor's:

$$M_k\, C_k = C_k + \kappa_k\, C_{k-1},$$

where $M_k$ is the compute multiplier, $C_k$ the compute of rung $k$ and $C_{k-1}$ that of the rung below. $\kappa = 1$: the donor's compute carries over in full.

  • The first hop carries the donor over in full ($\kappa = 1.04$). Later hops carry over less: 0.74 and 0.59. Since the donor's share of the rung grows (each hop adds 2 layers, so later hops are relatively smaller), the saving stays near 20%.
  • Only the last donor counts. The grown 12L can start from the grown 10L (which itself holds the 8L root's compute and is 0.032 better) or from the scratch 10L. Both end at the same loss, 2.3934 and 2.3935, so $\kappa$ is 0.74 and 0.73. The advantage the grown 10L carries does not reach the 12L: what counts is the compute of the rung grown from, not the whole chain. (One seed for the control; the two values differ by much less than the seed noise.)

If $\kappa$ were known, the grown law would follow from the scratch law. It is not constant here, so for now we predict the grown ladder with its own fit.

5The transfer rate does not depend on the budget

If $R$ is a property of the hop and not of the budget, the grown ladder's gains are predictable from the scratch ladder's steps. 6Smaller steps between rungs

One hop adds two layers. What if the rungs are closer together? With the older recipe (sign-rule balancing) at TPP 50, starting every chain from the same scratch 8L (test loss 2.8260), we grew three chains to 12 layers and trained scratch runs at every depth from 9 to 12:

  • dense: 8 → 9 → 10 → 11 → 12, one layer per hop;
  • medium: 8 → 10 → 12, two layers per hop (as in the main ladder);
  • sparse: 8 → 12 in one hop.

Each grown run trains on its own full TPP-50 budget, as in the main ladder.

Two things follow. A hop's gain does not grow from rung to rung, but it does not fade either: each rung passes on about the same advantage, as in Section 4. And a denser chain gives more at the top. A denser ladder has more rungs and so costs more in total, but every rung is also another point for the fit.

Caveat: the scratch runs of this study used the grown ladder's batch rule, which at 10 layers costs scratch about 0.0035 against its best batch (the fit in Section 1: 2.5529 at batch 72 vs 2.5494 at 45). The gains here are inflated by about that much. Comparisons between chains are not affected.

8Loss collapses and load balancing

Our first ladder balanced the experts with the common sign-rule bias update (each expert's routing bias moves by a fixed step toward its share of tokens). Its scratch runs at 10 layers and deeper sometimes collapsed: the training loss suddenly jumped by 1 to 6 nats, then came back. We count a collapse when a logged loss exceeds the median of the previous 50 logged values by more than 1 nat (consecutive points count as one event).

Sign-rule balancing only. Collapses per run (main ladder, density study) or summed over the runs of a cell (10L tuning grid; the grown 10L and 12L cells of the density study sum all chains at that depth).

  • More with depth, never in a grown run. At TPP 50: 0, 0, 1, 4 and 13 collapses from 8 to 12 layers; none in 24 grown runs.
  • What happens: layer 2 suddenly writes a huge output, every token's hidden state collapses onto one direction, and the routers of all layers collapse with it.
  • Quantile Balancing removes them. We rebuilt both ladders with it, with the same LR and batch rules: none of the 13 finished runs (8–14L, both ladders, both seeds) has a collapse.

This matters for the comparison. With sign-rule balancing, part of the grown ladder's gain could have come from avoiding collapses. In the Quantile Balancing ladder nothing collapses, and the gains of Section 2 (0.032 and 0.025 at 10 and 12 layers) are close to the first ladder's (0.034 and 0.026). The gain is growth, not collapse avoidance.

Limits and next steps

  • Few rungs. The laws rest on 4 scratch and 3 grown rungs, so the floor $E$ is not identified; both fits are pure power laws for now. One test so far (14L, 2.5× past the fit).
  • Seeds. Two seeds per ladder at 8–12L give the seed noise ($\sigma = 0.0019$); 14L and larger have one seed.
  • Downstream. Small models, near chance on some tasks.
  • Next. 16L and 18L for both ladders (running), scored against the predictions above; whether $\kappa$ keeps falling, and what sets it.
