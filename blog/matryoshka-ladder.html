Grow, Don't Restart: A Matryoshka Scaling Ladder and Its Scaling Law

Abstract

A scaling ladder trains a series of models of growing size and fits a law to their losses. We build the ladder differently. Each rung is grown from the trained rung below by an exact expansion that leaves the model's output unchanged, and is then trained on its own token budget. A ladder trains every rung anyway, so this Matryoshka ladder uses the same compute as a ladder trained from scratch. On mixture-of-experts models with 79M to 262M active parameters, with both ladders tuned the same way, grown rungs end with lower test loss than scratch rungs of the same size: by 0.034 at 10 layers and 0.026 at 12 layers. That is about 20% of what one more rung buys; the scratch law would need 1.3× the compute to match it. This share stays at 0.20 ± 0.01 when we change the token budget fourfold. So far the grown rungs follow a power law with nearly the same slope as the scratch rungs. We committed predictions for 14, 16 and 18 layers before training them. Along the way we found that scratch runs at 10 layers and deeper have sudden loss collapses; grown runs have had none.

The idea

To predict how a recipe behaves at large scale, one trains a ladder: the same recipe at a few growing sizes (the rungs), each on a token budget that grows with its size. One fits a power law to the final losses and reads off larger sizes. Every rung starts from random weights, so each rung relearns what the rung below already knows.

A Matryoshka ladder reuses that work. Rung $k{+}1$ starts as the trained rung $k$, expanded to the larger shape so that it computes exactly the same function (its loss right after growth equals the smaller model's), and is then trained on rung $k{+}1$'s full token budget. Each rung contains the one below, like nested dolls. (The name is unrelated to Matryoshka representation learning or MatFormer.)

Both ladders train every rung, on the same tokens per parameter, so they cost the same. If grown rungs end lower, the gain is free. The question this post is about: does the grown ladder still follow a scaling law that predicts its larger rungs? It does not have to follow the scratch law; it needs a law of its own.

We measure the gain at a rung with two numbers:

$$G_k = L^{\text{s}}_k - L^{\text{g}}_k, \qquad R_k = \frac{G_k}{L^{\text{s}}_{k-1} - L^{\text{s}}_k},$$

where $L^{\text{s}}_k$ and $L^{\text{g}}_k$ are the final test losses (nats per token) of the scratch and grown runs at rung $k$, $G_k$ is the gain, and $R_k$ the transfer rate: the gain as a share of what one scratch rung buys. $R = 0$: growth helps nothing. $R = 1$: growth is worth a whole rung.

Setup

Model. A mixture-of-experts (MoE) decoder. Every layer has attention and an MoE block with 128 experts, 8 chosen per token, each expert a quarter of the hidden size wide. Hidden size is $128 \times$ layers; attention heads are 64 wide with 2 key/value heads at every size. Attention uses a sliding window of 512 tokens, except one global layer in every four; output logits are soft-capped at 20. Data: FineWeb-Edu, Llama-3 tokenizer, sequences of 16,384 tokens, attention restarted at every document.

Optimizer. MuonH for weight matrices and AdamH for the output head: Muon and Adam steps with every matrix kept at a fixed norm (on a hypersphere), so no weight decay. Learning rates transfer across depth and token count with HyperP (arXiv 2603.28743): the peak hidden-layer LR is

$$\eta = \eta_{\text{ref}} \sqrt{\frac{8}{L}} \left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{0.32},$$

where $L$ is the number of layers, $T$ the run's training tokens, $T_0 = 15.73$B tokens the reference, and $\eta_{\text{ref}}$ the reference LR, set per ladder in Section 1. 100 warm-up steps, then cosine decay to 1% of the peak.

Budget. Every run trains on 100 tokens per active non-embedding parameter (TPP 100). Every run is scored on the same fixed test set: 3,840 sequences of 16,384 tokens (63M tokens).

Growing a rung exactly

One hop adds layers (8 → 10), hidden width (1024 → 1280), query heads and expert width, while the 128 experts and the 2 key/value heads stay. The grown model computes the same function as the trained small one:

  • New hidden dimensions start at zero. The embedding and every residual write leave them at zero, and the RMSNorm gains are scaled by $\sqrt{h/h'}$ ($h, h'$: old and new width), so the old dimensions are normalized exactly as before.
  • New query heads and new expert neurons read but do not write: their output weights are zero.
  • Old residual branches are scaled by $\sqrt{L'/L}$ ($L, L'$: old and new depth). This undoes the smaller branch multiplier $1/\sqrt{2L'}$ that the recipe gives deeper models.
  • New layers output zero. They are added after the old ones. Their query heads and expert neurons come in pairs with the same input weights and opposite output weights, so each pair cancels. (Plain zero weights do not work: MuonH keeps every matrix at its initial norm, which must not be zero.)

All other new weights (what new parts read, the new layers' inputs) keep the fresh initialization a scratch run would have. Check, growing a trained 10-layer model to 12 layers: on 384 test sequences the loss is 2.55759 before and 2.55761 after growth (difference $2\times10^{-5} \pm 2\times10^{-5}$), the KL divergence from the small model is 0.001, and the top-1 prediction agrees on 98.4% of tokens: the same function up to bf16 rounding.

The grown model then trains like any run: new optimizer state, its own warm-up and cosine schedule, its own batch and LR rule, and the data stream from the start. Ladder data is nested, so the larger rung's tokens include all of the smaller rung's.

1Tuning both ladders the same way

A grown model starts partly trained, so it may want a different LR and batch than a scratch model. Comparing both at one setting would favour whichever ladder it suits. We compare best to best: each ladder gets the same tuning effort, at a small rung, and the rules found there are applied unchanged to the larger rungs.

Grid. At 10 layers, TPP 100, we trained each ladder at batch 36, 72 and 144 and 2–4 learning rates per batch (7 scratch runs, 8 grown runs; every grown run starts from the same trained 8-layer model). We fit a quadratic surface to the final test loss,

$$\ell(x, y) = c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 y + c_3 x^2 + c_4 xy + c_5 y^2,$$

where $x = \ln(\eta_{\text{ref}}/0.012)$ is the log LR multiplier, $y = \ln B$ the log batch ($B$ in sequences), and $c_0, \ldots, c_5$ are least-squares coefficients. The best LR at batch $B$ solves $\partial\ell/\partial x = 0$, which gives a power law in $B$; the best batch is the lowest point along that line.

Do the rules transfer? Two checks at other depths:

  • 8 layers, scratch. The rule predicts the best LR at batch 48 to be ×0.90. An LR scan at batch 48 puts it at ×0.83 (quadratic through 4 LRs).
  • 12 layers, both ladders. Raising the LR to 1.41× the rule's value makes both worse: scratch 2.4270 vs 2.4191, grown 2.3956 vs 2.3930. The checks at 0.71× are running.

3Scaling laws and pre-registered predictions

Each ladder gets its own law:

$$L(C) = E + A\, C^{-\alpha}, \qquad C = 6ND,$$

where $L$ is the final test loss, $C$ the training compute in FLOPs, $N$ the active non-embedding parameters, $D$ the training tokens, $E$ an irreducible floor, and $A$, $\alpha$ the scale and slope. The scratch law is fitted to the scratch rungs; the grown law to the grown rungs only (the 8L root is a scratch run).

Before any 14-layer run started, we wrote down the fitting protocol and committed predictions under it (12 November 2026). The protocol fixes which runs enter, the law, how $E$ is estimated, the intervals and a pass rule; it will not change when the results come in. With few rungs it says:

  • Scratch (3 rungs): fewer than 5 rungs, so a pure power law ($E = 0$) is the main fit, as in HyperP. Result: $A = 20.54$, $\alpha = 0.0473$. A free floor gives the same fit: the best $E$ sits at its lower bound 0, and anything in $[0, 1.36]$ fits within the 95% range, so $E$ is not identified yet.
  • Grown (2 rungs): too few for a fit of its own, so it is fitted jointly with the scratch ladder with one shared floor $E$ and its own $A$ and $\alpha$. The shared floor also lands on 0, so the grown law is a pure power law too: $A = 19.52$, $\alpha = 0.0465$. The slopes differ by $-0.0009 \pm 0.0022$.

Status. 14L–18L have not been trained. The predictions above were committed before any of them started, and will be scored as they finish. Three scratch points and two grown points fit any smooth curve; the 14L runs are the first real test of either law.

4The transfer rate does not depend on the budget

If $R$ is a property of the hop and not of the budget, the grown ladder's gains are predictable from the scratch ladder's steps. An earlier study (older recipe: batch 192 at every rung, one LR rule for both arms) grew 8L into 10L at three budgets, 50, 100 and 200 tokens per parameter, each 8L donor trained at the same budget as the 10L runs.

5Smaller steps between rungs

One hop adds two layers. What if the rungs are closer together? At TPP 50, starting every chain from the same scratch 8L (test loss 2.8260), we grew three chains to 12 layers and trained scratch runs at every depth from 9 to 12:

  • dense: 8 → 9 → 10 → 11 → 12, one layer per hop;
  • medium: 8 → 10 → 12, two layers per hop (as in the main ladder);
  • sparse: 8 → 12 in one hop.

Each grown run trains on its own full TPP-50 budget, as in the main ladder.

Two things follow. A hop's gain does not grow from rung to rung, but it does not fade either: each rung passes on about the same advantage. And a denser chain gives more at the top. A denser ladder has more rungs and so costs more in total, but every rung is also another point for the fit.

Caveat: the scratch runs of this study used the grown ladder's batch rule, which at 10 layers costs scratch about 0.0035 against its best batch (the fit in Section 1: 2.5529 at batch 72 vs 2.5494 at 45). The gains here are inflated by about that much. Comparisons between chains are not affected.

7Loss collapses in scratch runs

Scratch runs at 10 layers and deeper sometimes collapse: the training loss suddenly jumps by 1 to 6 nats, then comes back. We count a collapse when a logged loss exceeds the median of the previous 50 logged values by more than 1 nat (consecutive points count as one event).

Collapses per run (main ladder, density study) or summed over the runs of a cell (10L tuning grid; the grown 10L and 12L cells of the density study sum all chains at that depth).

  • None at 8 layers, more with depth. At TPP 50: 0, 0, 1, 4 and 13 collapses from 8 to 12 layers.
  • Never in a grown run: 24 grown runs, at every LR, batch, budget and depth we tried.
  • Smaller batches collapse more. In the 10L tuning grid: 3 collapses at batch 36, 4 at batch 72, none at batch 144; none of the five 10L scratch runs of the older recipe (batch 192) collapsed.
  • A higher LR does not fix it. 12L scratch at 1.41× the LR: 3 collapses and a final loss of 2.4270 vs 2.4191.

What happens at a collapse: layer 2 suddenly writes a huge output, every token's hidden state collapses onto one direction, and the routers of all layers collapse with it. Layer-1 routing is collapsed in every run, grown ones included, so that alone is not the cause. We are considering an architecture fix: a dense (non-MoE) first layer, as DeepSeek-V3 and Kimi use.

This matters for the comparison. The scratch runs keep their collapses in the fits and in the gains above (the protocol flags them but never drops them). If a collapse costs final loss, part of the grown ladder's gain comes from avoiding it. Growth does help where scratch does not collapse: in the density study neither 9L run collapsed, and the gain there was 0.058. At 10–12 layers we cannot yet separate the two effects.

Limits and next steps

  • Few rungs, one seed each. The laws rest on 3 scratch and 2 grown rungs; the floor $E$ is not identified, and the grown law borrows its floor from the joint fit. Seed noise (0.0007 per run) comes from one seed pair at TPP 50.
  • Collapses. The scratch rungs at 10L and 12L collapsed 1 and 4 times. Until a fix removes them, the gain mixes the effect of growth with the cost of collapses.
  • Downstream. Small models, near chance on some tasks.
  • Next. 14L, 16L and 18L for both ladders, scored against the predictions above; seeds at 8, 10 and 12 layers; the 12L LR checks at 0.71× (running); and a 12L grown from the scratch 10L, to tell one hop's gain from the gain built up over two hops.
